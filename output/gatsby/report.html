📖 Plot Summary

In the spring of 1922, Henry C. Gatz recounts his arrival in West Egg and his introduction to the glittering yet hollow world of Long Island’s elite through his neighbor, the enigmatic Jay Gatsby. He is drawn into the orbit of Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom, whose wealth masks deep moral decay, and meets Jordan Baker, a detached and dishonest golfer who becomes his reluctant companion. Through Jordan, Gatz learns that Gatsby’s lavish parties and mysterious persona are all orchestrated to rekindle a long-lost romance with Daisy, whom he met five years earlier as a poor officer named James Gatz. Gatsby, once a dreamer from North Dakota, reinvented himself entirely—adopting wealth, status, and an Oxford pedigree—to win back the woman who had married Tom while he was away at war. Gatz becomes the reluctant intermediary in Gatsby’s plan to reunite with Daisy, hosting their fateful tea, where old feelings resurface amid the opulence of Gatsby’s mansion. As summer turns to heat, tensions escalate when Tom Buchanan confronts Gatsby at the Plaza Hotel, exposing his criminal ties to Meyer Wolfsheim and shattering his illusion of legitimacy. Daisy, overwhelmed, admits she once loved both men but ultimately retreats into the safety of her marriage, leaving Gatsby shattered. The turning point arrives when Myrtle Wilson, Tom’s mistress, is killed by a speeding yellow car—driven by Daisy but owned by Gatsby. Tom, in a cruel act of betrayal, tells George Wilson that Gatsby was the driver, inciting Wilson’s vengeful pursuit. Meanwhile, Gatsby, still clinging to the belief that Daisy will choose him, waits vigilantly outside her house, taking blame for the accident to protect her. His dream, already frayed by her indifference and the vulgarity of his own world, collapses as he remains alone in the moonlight, watching a house where his idealized past has already died. After Gatsby’s murder at the hands of the grieving Wilson, Henry C. Gatz is left to navigate the hollow aftermath: no one attends the funeral except Gatsby’s father, who arrives with a childhood notebook revealing the boy’s relentless self-improvement and quiet ambition. Gatz is stunned by the depth of Gatsby’s longing and the emptiness of his legacy—guests vanish, Wolfsheim refuses involvement, and even Klipspringer only calls to retrieve his tennis shoes. As Gatz reflects on the scene, he sees Gatsby not as a fraud but as a tragic idealist who believed in the green light across the bay—the unattainable dream of renewal, love, and self-reinvention. In the final moments, standing on the beach where Gatsby once reached toward that distant glow, Gatz observes how all people are pushed back by time and circumstance, yet still yearn forward, forever caught between the past they cherish and the future they can never reclaim.

Themes: identity, ambition, loss
