📖 Plot Summary

Nick Carraway, a young Midwesterner seeking opportunity in the East, moves to West Egg in the summer of 1922 and becomes entangled in the lives of his wealthy cousin Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom, whose marriage is strained by infidelity and moral decay. Through Daisy, Nick meets the enigmatic Jordan Baker, and through their social circles, he is drawn into the orbit of Jay Gatsby, his mysterious neighbor whose lavish parties mask a solitary obsession: rekindling a long-lost romance with Daisy. Gatsby, once James Gatz, has reinvented himself entirely—building wealth through illicit means, acquiring an opulent mansion across the bay from Daisy, and accumulating symbols of status—all to win back the woman he loved five years prior. Nick, moved by Gatsby’s quiet idealism, agrees to facilitate a reunion between Gatsby and Daisy, reigniting a fragile connection that seems to revive Gatsby’s hope. As Gatsby and Daisy rekindle their affair, tensions escalate between Gatsby and Tom, culminating in a dramatic confrontation at the Plaza Hotel in Manhattan. Tom exposes Gatsby’s criminal ties to Meyer Wolfsheim and undermines his fabricated aristocratic past, while Daisy, overwhelmed, admits she once loved both men but ultimately cannot abandon her life with Tom. That same day, Myrtle Wilson, Tom’s mistress, is killed by a speeding yellow car—driven by Daisy, though Gatsby takes full blame to protect her. In the aftermath, George Wilson, Myrtle’s grieving husband, is manipulated by Tom into believing Gatsby was both Myrtle’s lover and her murderer. Meanwhile, Nick watches helplessly as Gatsby clings to his dream, waiting outside Daisy’s house for a call that never comes, convinced she will choose him despite the chaos he has inherited. After Gatsby is shot and killed by Wilson in his pool, Nick is left to organize a funeral amid the cold indifference of Gatsby’s former guests and the silence of Daisy and Tom, who vanish without a trace. Only Gatsby’s father, Henry C. Gatz, arrives to mourn, revealing the boyish ambition behind the man—his self-improvement schedule, his reverence for Dan Cody, and his unwavering belief in self-reinvention. As Nick reflects on the emptiness of the East and the moral bankruptcy of its elite, he realizes Gatsby’s tragedy was not his crime or his wealth, but his faith in a dream that had already faded. In the novel’s final moments, Nick visits Gatsby’s abandoned mansion and stands on the beach, gazing at the green light across the bay—a symbol of hope now irretrievably lost—and meditates on the human tendency to strive against the current of time, forever pulled back into the past.

Chapter 1: I 5,892 words · 39m

The chapter opens with Nick Carraway, Nick Carraway, reflecting on his father's advice to reserve judgment, a habit that made him a confidant in college and shaped his moral outlook, though he later became disillusioned by the moral chaos he witnessed in the East—except for Gatsby, whom he uniquely admired for his extraordinary gift for hope. In the spring of 1922, Nick rents a modest eighty-dollar bungalow in West Egg, arriving alone after his roommate is sent to Washington; he feels isolated until a stranger asks him for directions, granting him a sense of belonging. That evening, he drives by car to East Egg to dine with his cousin Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom, a Yale acquaintance, at their grand Georgian Colonial mansion, where he meets the enigmatic Jordan Baker, who lies motionless on a couch. During dinner, Tom aggressively espouses racist pseudoscience from 'The Rise of the Coloured Empires,' and a telephone call reveals his affair with a woman in New York, which Jordan confirms to Nick; Daisy, visibly shaken, deflects with a fabricated story about a nightingale and later confesses her despair, wishing her daughter would be a 'beautiful little fool.'
