📖 Plot Summary

In the spring of 1922, Nick Carraway moves to West Egg, New York, seeking opportunity and self-improvement, renting a modest home next to the extravagant mansion of the mysterious Jay Gatsby. Through his cousin Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom—a wealthy, arrogant Yale alumnus—Nick is introduced to the decadent world of Long Island’s elite, including the cynical socialite Jordan Baker. He soon becomes fascinated by Gatsby, whose lavish parties draw crowds of strangers yet conceal the host’s solitary longing: a quiet, obsessive devotion to Daisy, whom Gatsby had loved five years earlier before the war. Nick learns that Gatsby bought his mansion solely to be across the bay from Daisy’s home, and after arranging a tense, emotional reunion between them, Gatsby believes he can recreate the past and reclaim her love. As Gatsby’s dream intensifies, tensions rise between him and Tom Buchanan, who grows suspicious of Gatsby’s wealth and background. During a sweltering afternoon in Manhattan, Tom exposes Gatsby’s criminal ties through Meyer Wolfsheim and forces Daisy to admit she once loved him but can no longer fully commit to Gatsby. On the drive back, Daisy, driving Gatsby’s yellow car, accidentally kills Myrtle Wilson, Tom’s mistress, in the Valley of Ashes. Tom, knowing Daisy was behind the wheel, manipulates George Wilson—Myrtle’s grief-stricken husband—into believing Gatsby was both her lover and her killer. Meanwhile, Gatsby, in a gesture of devotion, takes the blame for the accident and waits outside Daisy’s house, guarding her from Tom’s wrath. That night, consumed by grief and delusion, Wilson shoots Gatsby in his pool before turning the gun on himself. In the aftermath, Nick struggles to arrange a proper funeral for Gatsby, but not one of the hundreds who attended his parties shows up; only Gatsby’s father, a few servants, and a mysterious man with owl-eyed glasses attend. Tom Buchanan, unrepentant, confesses to Nick that he told Wilson Gatsby owned the car, justifying it as protection of his own interests. Nick, disillusioned by the moral emptiness of the East, realizes that Gatsby’s dream was noble in its hopefulness, even if tragically misplaced. He reflects on Gatsby’s belief in the green light across the bay—the symbol of an idealized future—and concludes that despite the corruption and carelessness surrounding him, Gatsby represented something rare: an enduring faith in possibility. Nick leaves New York, carrying with him the memory of a man who chased a dream too beautiful to survive the world he sought to win.

Themes: identity, ambition, loss

Chapter 1: I 5,892 words · 39m

In the spring of 1922, Nick Carraway arrives in West Egg and rents a modest eighty-dollar bungalow, acquiring an old Dodge, a Finnish housekeeper, and a dog that soon runs away; he immerses himself in twelve volumes on banking to become a 'well-rounded man.' Reflecting on his father’s advice to reserve judgment, he recalls how his tolerance made him a confidant in college and later led to disillusionment with the moral chaos of the East, except for Gatsby, whom he uniquely admired for his extraordinary gift for hope. After driving by car to East Egg to visit his cousin Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom—whom he knew at Yale—he is introduced to Jordan Baker, who lies motionless on a couch. During dinner at their grand Georgian Colonial mansion, Tom espouses racist views from The Rise of the Coloured Empires, a phone call reveals his affair with a woman in New York, and Daisy confesses her cynical belief that a girl’s best fate is to be a 'beautiful little fool,' followed by a chilling smirk. As Nick returns to his estate, he observes Gatsby standing alone on his lawn, arms outstretched toward a single green light at the end of a distant dock, trembling in the warm summer night before vanishing into the shadows.
