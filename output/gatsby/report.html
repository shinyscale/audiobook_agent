📖 Plot Summary

In the spring of 1922, Nick Carraway moves to West Egg on Long Island, seeking opportunity in the bond business and renting a modest bungalow next to the lavish mansion of the enigmatic Jay Gatsby. Through his cousin Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom—residents of elite East Egg—Nick is introduced to the glittering yet morally hollow world of the Jazz Age. He meets the cynical, independent Jordan Baker, who reveals Tom’s affair with Myrtle Wilson, a woman trapped in the desolate Valley of Ashes. As Nick observes the decadence and emotional detachment around him, he becomes fascinated by Gatsby, whose mysterious wealth and nightly parties draw crowds but conceal a singular, obsessive longing: to reunite with Daisy, whom he loved five years earlier as Lieutenant Jay Gatz before the war. Nick learns from Jordan that Gatsby bought his mansion solely so Daisy could see the green light at the end of her dock, and he agrees to arrange a quiet reunion between them. The reunion between Gatsby and Daisy rekindles their romance, but it quickly unravels under the weight of class and truth. Gatsby’s fabricated past—Oxford, war heroism, inherited fortune—is exposed by Tom Buchanan, who reveals Gatsby’s criminal ties to Meyer Wolfsheim and bootlegging operations. At the Plaza Hotel, Tom forces Daisy to choose between them; she wavers, then admits she once loved both men but cannot erase their shared history. That night, driving back in Gatsby’s yellow car, Daisy accidentally kills Myrtle Wilson, who mistakes the vehicle for Tom’s. Gatsby takes the blame, refusing to abandon his dream that Daisy will leave Tom. Meanwhile, Myrtle’s widower, George Wilson, misled by Tom into believing Gatsby was both Myrtle’s lover and her killer, descends into grief-fueled delusion, fixating on the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg as divine judgment. He tracks Gatsby to his mansion and shoots him while he floats alone in his pool, then turns the gun on himself. In the aftermath, Nick Carraway is left to mourn Gatsby’s isolation: no one attends his funeral except his father, a few servants, the owl-eyed man who admired his books, and a minister. Daisy and Tom vanish without a word, their wealth shielding them from consequence. Nick confronts Jordan Baker, who ends their relationship with accusations of dishonesty, and later encounters Tom, who coldly admits he told Wilson Gatsby owned the car—justifying it as necessary. Disillusioned by the moral decay of the East, Nick reflects on Gatsby’s tragic belief that the past could be repeated, symbolized by his reaching for the green light across the bay. In the final moments, Nick recalls the original, untamed beauty of the island and the human impulse to strive toward unreachable dreams, forever pulled back by the tide of time and history.

Themes: identity, ambition, loss
