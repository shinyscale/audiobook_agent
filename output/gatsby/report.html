📖 Plot Summary

In the spring of 1922, Henry C. Gatz, Henry C. Gatz, recounts his journey into the world of Jay Gatsby through the eyes of Nick Carraway, a Yale acquaintance who moves to West Egg and becomes entangled in the lives of the wealthy elite. Nick rents a modest home near the opulent mansion of the enigmatic Jay Gatsby, whose lavish parties draw crowds but conceal his true identity. Through Nick, Gatz learns that Gatsby’s wealth and spectacle are all orchestrated to win back Daisy Buchanan, Nick’s cousin, whom Gatsby loved five years prior as a penniless officer. Daisy, now married to the brutish and arrogant Tom Buchanan, lives across the bay in East Egg, and Gatsby’s entire life—his name, his fortune, even his Oxford education—has been meticulously constructed to bridge the gap between who he was and who he believes she still loves. Gatz, as Henry C. Gatz, pieces together this story from Nick’s observations, diary entries, and the testimonies of others, revealing Gatsby’s profound isolation beneath the glitter. As summer unfolds, Nick becomes Gatsby’s reluctant confidant, facilitating a reunion between Gatsby and Daisy that initially seems to fulfill Gatsby’s dream. But the illusion shatters under Tom’s scrutiny, exposing Gatsby’s criminal ties through Meyer Wolfsheim and his fabricated aristocracy. The tension culminates on a sweltering afternoon at the Plaza Hotel, where Gatsby demands Daisy renounce her love for Tom; she falters, revealing she once loved Tom but now loves Gatsby too—a confession that destroys Gatsby’s idealized vision of her. That night, Daisy, driving Gatsby’s car, strikes and kills Myrtle Wilson, Tom’s mistress, who had been mistaking the car for Tom’s. Tom, in a calculated act of betrayal, tells Myrtle’s grieving husband George Wilson that Gatsby owned the vehicle. Gatsby, refusing to abandon his dream, takes the blame, spending the night watching Daisy’s house while she and Tom retreat into their hollow, intimate silence, untouched by consequence. After Gatsby’s murder—gunned down in his pool by a grief-stricken Wilson, who then turns the gun on himself—Gatz, Henry C. Gatz, recounts the haunting emptiness of the aftermath. Daisy and Tom vanish without a trace; Gatsby’s so-called friends, including Wolfsheim and Klipspringer, disappear or offer only petty requests. Only Gatsby’s father, Henry C. Gatz, arrives from Minnesota, carrying his son’s childhood self-improvement schedule as proof of the boy’s boundless ambition. At the sparse funeral, attended by Nick, a minister, and the owl-eyed man who once marveled at Gatsby’s real books, Gatz reflects on the tragic irony: his son died believing in a dream that never existed, yet was more noble than the careless people who destroyed him. In the final moments, Gatz, alive and whole, walks the beach where Gatsby once reached for the green light, now extinguished, and understands that humanity, like Gatsby, is forever chasing a past it cannot reclaim.

Chapter 1: I 5,892 words · 39m

In the spring of 1922, Nick Carraway arrives in West Egg and rents a modest eighty-dollar bungalow, settling alone after his roommate is transferred to Washington; he acquires an old Dodge, a Finnish housekeeper, and briefly a dog that runs away, initially feeling isolated until a stranger’s request for directions makes him feel like a local guide. He buys twelve volumes on banking and investment as symbols of future success and drives to East Egg to visit his cousin Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom, whom he knew at Yale, where they reside in a lavish Georgian mansion overlooking the bay. During dinner, Tom aggressively espouses racist pseudoscience from 'The Rise of the Coloured Empires,' while Daisy and Jordan Baker respond with detached irony; a phone call reveals Tom’s affair in New York, which Jordan confirms to Nick, and Daisy bitterly wishes her daughter be a 'beautiful little fool,' as the evening dissolves into artificial rituals like pointless candle-relighting. After leaving, Nick drives home to West Egg, reflecting on Daisy’s emotional detachment and Tom’s depression, before seeing Gatsby standing alone on his lawn, arms outstretched toward a distant green light at the end of a dock, only for Gatsby to vanish into the night.
