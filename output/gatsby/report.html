📖 Plot Summary

Nick Carraway, a young Midwesterner seeking moral clarity, moves to West Egg in the spring of 1922 and rents a modest home next to the extravagant mansion of the enigmatic Jay Gatsby. Through his cousin Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom—wealthy, arrogant East Egg elites—he is introduced to the glittering yet hollow world of Long Island’s elite. He soon becomes entangled in Gatsby’s obsessive quest to rekindle a lost romance with Daisy, whom Gatsby met five years earlier as a young officer before she married Tom. Gatsby, who has amassed his fortune through dubious means and reinvented himself from James Gatz of North Dakota, bought his mansion solely so Daisy could see the green light at the end of her dock. Nick, initially drawn to Gatsby’s idealism and unique capacity for hope, becomes the reluctant intermediary in their reunion, arranging a tense tea that reignites their connection and reawakens Gatsby’s delusion that he can erase time and reclaim the past. As Gatsby and Daisy rekindle their affair, tensions escalate between Gatsby and Tom, culminating in a volatile confrontation at the Plaza Hotel in New York. Tom exposes Gatsby’s criminal ties to Meyer Wolfsheim and undermines his claims of privilege, forcing Daisy to admit she once loved Tom—a confession that shatters Gatsby’s dream. That same afternoon, Myrtle Wilson, Tom’s mistress, is killed by a speeding yellow car driven by Daisy, though Gatsby takes responsibility to protect her. George Wilson, Myrtle’s grief-stricken husband, misled by Tom into believing Gatsby was both Myrtle’s lover and her killer, hunts down Gatsby at his mansion. Meanwhile, Nick watches helplessly as Daisy and Tom retreat into their wealth and indifference, reuniting in silent complicity after the tragedy, leaving Gatsby vulnerable and alone. In the aftermath, Gatsby is shot and killed by Wilson in his pool, a victim of his own idealism and the moral decay around him. Nick struggles to organize a funeral, only to find that Gatsby’s so-called friends—those who feasted at his parties and spread rumors about him—abandon him entirely. Only Gatsby’s father, Henry C. Gatz, arrives, proud of his son’s self-made legacy revealed in a childhood book filled with self-improvement goals. As Nick reflects on the emptiness of the East, he realizes that Gatsby’s tragedy was not merely personal but symbolic: a man who believed in the green light of the future, only to be crushed by a world that had no place for such dreams. With disillusioned resolve, Nick returns to the Midwest, having witnessed the hollowness of wealth and the cost of clinging to illusions, leaving behind a landscape where moral order has been replaced by careless privilege and silent complicity.

Themes: identity, ambition, loss
