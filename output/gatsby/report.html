📖 Plot Summary

Jay Gatsby, reflecting from the vantage of time, recounts his journey from James Gatz of North Dakota to the enigmatic millionaire of West Egg, driven by an unwavering dream to reclaim a lost love. In the spring of 1922, he buys a grand mansion across the bay from Daisy Buchanan—his former flame from Louisville in 1917—so that he might one day see the green light at the end of her dock. Through Nick Carraway, his neighbor and reluctant confidant, Gatsby orchestrates a reunion with Daisy, rekindling their romance beneath the opulence of his parties and the illusion of his fabricated aristocratic past. Yet beneath the glittering surface, Gatsby’s identity is built on myth: his Oxford education, war medals, and wealth are all carefully curated to match the world Daisy inhabits, even as he clings to the belief that the past can be repeated and love restored exactly as it was. As Gatsby’s dream intensifies, the world around him begins to fracture. Daisy, now married to the brutish Tom Buchanan, is pulled between her lingering affection for Gatsby and the security of her privileged life. Their affair becomes public during a scorching summer day at the Plaza Hotel, where Daisy’s confession that she once loved Tom—and her subsequent retreat into silence—shatters Gatsby’s illusion. The collision of their worlds leads to tragedy: Myrtle Wilson, Tom’s mistress, is killed by a speeding yellow car, though Daisy is behind the wheel. Gatsby, refusing to let go, takes the blame, believing Daisy will choose him in the aftermath. But as he waits for her call from his mansion, Daisy retreats into Tom’s embrace, leaving Gatsby exposed and alone. Meanwhile, George Wilson, Myrtle’s grieving husband, misled by Tom into believing Gatsby was both Myrtle’s lover and her killer, seeks vengeance. In the quiet dawn after the storm, Gatsby stands watch outside Daisy’s house, still holding onto the hope that she will reach out—until his dream dissolves with the morning light. He is shot by Wilson at his pool, a victim not of crime but of the impossible ideal he spent his life pursuing. Though he survives to tell this story, the weight of his loss lingers in every word. Nick Carraway, the only one who truly understood him, organizes a funeral that no one attends except Gatsby’s father, a humble man who reads from his son’s childhood schedule with quiet pride. As Gatsby reflects on the emptiness of the East and the moral decay of its elite, he sees himself not as a fool, but as a man who dared to believe in something pure. He ends his tale by gazing at the bay, remembering how every boat is borne back ceaselessly into the past—not because it fails, but because hope, even when broken, is the only thing that moves us forward.
