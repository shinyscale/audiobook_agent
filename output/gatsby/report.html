📖 Plot Summary

Nick Carraway, a Midwesterner disillusioned by the East, moves to West Egg in the spring of 1922 and rents a modest home beside the lavish mansion of the enigmatic Jay Gatsby. Through his cousin Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom—wealthy, arrogant residents of East Egg—Nick is introduced to the glittering yet hollow world of Jazz Age excess. He also meets the cynical, independent Jordan Baker, who reveals Tom’s affair with Myrtle Wilson, a woman trapped in the desolate Valley of Ashes. As Nick becomes increasingly drawn into Gatsby’s orbit, he learns that Gatsby’s opulent parties and mysterious persona are all orchestrated to win back Daisy, the woman he loved five years earlier before the war. Gatsby’s wealth, built through bootlegging and criminal ties under the mentorship of Meyer Wolfsheim, is a performance designed to make him worthy of Daisy, whose voice he once described as full of money. The rising action centers on Gatsby’s desperate attempt to rekindle his romance with Daisy, facilitated by Nick’s reluctant mediation. After a tense, emotional reunion at Nick’s cottage, Gatsby and Daisy are briefly reconnected, but their fragile rekindling is shattered when Tom Buchanan confronts Gatsby at the Plaza Hotel. There, Gatsby’s fabricated past collapses under scrutiny, and Daisy, overwhelmed and uncertain, admits she once loved both men but ultimately cannot abandon the security Tom provides. The confrontation ends with Tom forcing them to return to Long Island in Gatsby’s car, while Myrtle Wilson, mistaking the vehicle for Tom’s, runs into the road and is killed. Though Daisy was driving, Gatsby resolves to take the blame, shielding her from consequence. Meanwhile, Myrtle’s husband George Wilson, misled by Tom into believing Gatsby was both Myrtle’s lover and her killer, descends into grief-fueled madness, fixating on the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg as divine judgment. The climax arrives with Gatsby’s murder: Wilson tracks him to his mansion and shoots him in the pool before taking his own life. Nick is left alone to arrange Gatsby’s funeral, only to find that the society Gatsby cultivated has vanished—no guests attend, no calls are returned, not even Meyer Wolfsheim or Jordan Baker. Gatsby’s father arrives from Minnesota, revealing the boyhood dreams and self-improvement logs that shaped the man Gatsby became, underscoring the tragic gap between aspiration and reality. Nick, disillusioned by the East’s moral decay, reflects on the American Dream’s corruption, comparing Gatsby’s futile pursuit of Daisy to the original Dutch sailors’ awe at an unspoiled New World. He realizes Gatsby’s dream was not merely for a woman but for a perfect, unreachable past, and as he stands on the beach one last time, he sees the green light across the bay as a symbol of all human striving—eternally receding, forever pulled back into the past. Nick departs for the Midwest, having survived the chaos with his moral compass intact, though deeply scarred.

Chapter 1: I 5,892 words · 39m

The chapter opens with Nick Carraway reflecting on his father’s advice to reserve judgment due to unequal life advantages, a principle that made him a confidant to college peers but left him disillusioned after returning from the East last autumn, except for Gatsby, whom he views as uniquely hopeful and gifted with romantic sensitivity. In the spring of 1922, Nick rents a modest eighty-dollar bungalow in West Egg, overlooking Long Island Sound between two grand estates, one belonging to the mysterious Jay Gatsby; after feeling isolated, he gains a sense of belonging when a stranger asks him for directions to West Egg village, and he purchases twelve volumes on banking as symbols of his ambition to become a 'well-rounded man.' He drives by car to East Egg to visit his cousin Daisy Buchanan and her husband Tom, a domineering Yale acquaintance, at their opulent Georgian Colonial home, where he meets the enigmatic Jordan Baker. During an evening dinner marked by tension, Tom aggressively promotes racist theories from 'The Rise of the Coloured Empires,' a phone call interrupts the meal prompting Daisy to fabricate a story about the butler’s nose, and she later reveals her despair by declaring she hopes her daughter will be a 'beautiful little fool,' while Jordan confides in Nick that Tom has a mistress in New York.
