THE ADVENTURES OF SHERWOOD MANOR

by A. Sample Author


PROLOGUE

The old manor had stood for three centuries, watching generations come and go like seasons. Lady Margaret Ashworth often said it had a soul of its own.


Chapter 1: The Arrival

It was a truth universally acknowledged that young Edmund Blackwood was not the sort of gentleman who arrived anywhere on time. Yet on this particular Tuesday in September, he found himself standing before Sherwood Manor a full quarter-hour early.

"Remarkable," muttered his companion, Dr. Heinrich Müller, adjusting his spectacles. The German physician had known Edmund since their university days at Cambridge.

Edmund straightened his cravat. "Don't look so shocked, Heinrich. Even I can be punctual when the circumstances demand it."

"And what circumstances would those be?" asked Heinrich.

Before Edmund could answer, the great oak doors swung open to reveal a woman of perhaps sixty years, silver hair swept into an elegant chignon. This was Mrs. Catherine Pemberton, the manor's housekeeper for some thirty years.

"Mr. Blackwood, Dr. Müller," she said, her voice crisp as autumn leaves. "Lady Ashworth is expecting you in the drawing room."

* * *

The drawing room was exactly as Edmund remembered from his childhood visits—Persian carpets, ancestral portraits, and the faint smell of lavender and old books. Lady Margaret sat by the fire, her arthritic hands folded in her lap.

"Edmund, dear boy," she said. "You look more like your father every day."

"A dubious compliment, Aunt Margaret."

She laughed—a sound like wind chimes. "Sir Reginald Blackwood was many things, but he was never boring. Now sit, both of you. We have much to discuss."

Heinrich took the chair by the window while Edmund settled onto the settee. Outside, the Yorkshire moors stretched endlessly toward the horizon.

"It concerns the Montague inheritance," Lady Margaret began.


Chapter 2: The Letter

Dear Diary,

October 15th, 1887

The strangest thing happened today. A letter arrived from Constantinople—from someone calling themselves "V." The handwriting was elegant, almost feminine, yet the tone suggested military precision.

I showed it to Edmund, of course. He turned quite pale.

"Constantinople," he murmured. "I thought that business was finished."

I haven't seen him look so troubled since the incident at Château Beaumont.

Yours in confusion,
Margaret


Chapter 3: Revelations

"You cannot be serious!" Edmund slammed his fist upon the mahogany table. "The Vasiliev documents were destroyed. I watched them burn myself."

Lady Margaret remained implacable. "Apparently not all of them, dear nephew."

Dr. Müller produced a yellowed envelope from his coat pocket. "This arrived at my practice in München last week. Identical handwriting. See for yourself."

Edmund snatched the letter and read:

Dear Dr. Müller,

What was buried at Père Lachaise does not sleep quietly. The Comte de Rochefort's secrets will surface with the spring thaw.

Your old friend from Heidelberg sends his regards.

V.

"Père Lachaise," Edmund whispered. "The cemetery in Paris."

"The very same," said Heinrich. "I believe we must pay a visit to Monsieur Jean-Pierre Beaumont."

* * *

Jean-Pierre Beaumont lived in a cramped apartment above a boulangerie in Montmartre. The smell of fresh bread did little to mask the underlying odor of pipe tobacco and desperation.

"Messieurs," he said, ushering them inside. "I have been expecting you."

Edmund noted the man's trembling hands, the dark circles beneath his eyes. Jean-Pierre had aged twenty years since their last meeting in Lyon.

"The Comte's widow," Jean-Pierre began. "She knows everything."


Chapter 4: The Widow's Tale

Marie-Claire de Rochefort received them in her salon overlooking the Bois de Boulogne. At forty-three, she retained the striking beauty that had made her the toast of Second Empire Paris.

"My husband was many things," she said, pouring tea with practiced grace. "A brilliant mathematician. A devoted father to our children, Étienne and Marguerite. But above all, he was a keeper of secrets."

Edmund leaned forward. "What manner of secrets, Madame?"

"The kind men kill for, Mr. Blackwood." Her eyes met his. "And die for."


EPILOGUE

Six months later, Edmund stood once more before Sherwood Manor. The mystery of the Vasiliev documents had been solved—at a cost he was only beginning to understand.

Lady Margaret was gone, buried in the family plot beside Sir Reginald. Mrs. Pemberton had retired to a cottage in Devon. And Heinrich... Heinrich had returned to München, changed in ways Edmund could not quite articulate.

"Some doors," Edmund murmured to himself, "should remain closed."

But even as he spoke, he knew the words for a lie. There would always be another mystery, another shadow requiring illumination.

It was, after all, his nature.


THE END
